## Quick Review of RTP and RTSP

- **RTSP** is a control protocol that lets a client send commands (e.g., *pause*, *forward*, *stop*) to a server to manage video playback.
- RTSP runs over **TCP**, similar to HTTP.
- **RTP** carries the actual media data and runs over **UDP**.

## Real‑Time Control Protocol (RTCP)
- **RTCP** stands for *Real‑Time Control Protocol*.
- It works **in conjunction with RTP**; it does not operate independently.
- Each participant in an RTP session periodically sends RTCP control packets to all other participants.

### What RTCP Provides

- Reports and statistics such as:
- Number of packets sent
- Number of packets lost
- Inter‑arrival jitter
- Delay and variation of delay (jitter)
- These metrics allow the sender and receiver to **adjust transmission parameters** (e.g., bitrate, sending rate).

### Feedback Loop

- The feedback from RTCP is used by the opposite side (client ↔ server) to modify its transmission behavior.
- Example: If a client reports high packet loss, the server may reduce its sending rate.

## Multicast in RTP

- RTP can use **multicast addresses** to send the same media stream to multiple receivers simultaneously (one sender, many receivers).

## Port Numbers

- RTP and RTCP use **different port numbers** to keep their traffic separate.
- This separation prevents RTCP control traffic from interfering with the RTP data stream.

## Bandwidth Management for RTCP

- RTCP traffic is intentionally kept **smaller than the RTP data traffic**.
- As the number of participants in a conference grows, each participant’s allocated RTCP bandwidth **decreases** to avoid overwhelming the network.
- Example scaling:
- With 2 participants, a certain bandwidth is reserved for RTCP.
- Adding a 3rd, 4th, etc., participant reduces the per‑participant RTCP bandwidth proportionally.

These notes capture the key concepts of RTP, RTSP, and RTCP, their interactions, and how they manage control information and network resources in real‑time streaming applications.

## RTP/RTCP Overview
- The sender transmits **RTP** packets for media data and **RTCP** packets that carry control information.
- RTCP packets contain reports on **packet loss**, **delay**, and other quality metrics.
- Clients (receivers) also send RTCP packets to provide feedback to the sender.

## RTCP Packet Types
- **Receiver Report (RR)** – sent by a receiver.
- **Sender Report (SR)** – sent by a sender.
- **Source Description (SDES)** – provides descriptive information about the source.

## Receiver Report (RR)
- Includes the following fields:
- **Fraction of packets lost**
- **Highest sequence number received** (last sequence number)
- **Average inter‑arrival jitter**
- This information helps the sender adjust transmission parameters.

## Sender Report (SR)
- Contains a **Synchronization Source identifier (SSRC)**:
- Each participant that generates a stream chooses a random 32‑bit SSRC and registers it.
- The SSRC is placed in **every RTP packet** and in all RTCP packets so that receivers can associate packets with the correct source.
- Provides transmission statistics:
- **Current NTP timestamp** (wall‑clock time)
- **RTP timestamp** of the most recently sent RTP packet
- **Number of RTP packets sent**
- **Number of RTP payload bytes sent**

## Source Description (SDES) Packets
- Carry human‑readable information about the source:
- **CNAME (canonical name)** – a unique identifier for the source.
- **User name**
- **Email address**
- **SSRC** – links the descriptive fields to the specific stream.
- The SDES packet creates a mapping between the SSRC and the user’s identity, allowing participants to know who is sending each stream.

## Synchronizing Multiple Media Streams
- RTCP can synchronize different media streams (e.g., audio and video) within a single RTCP session.
- In a video‑conferencing application:
- One RTCP stream is generated for **video**.
- Another RTCP stream is generated for **audio**.
- The timestamps carried in RTCP are tied to the **media sampling clock** of each stream, not to the local device’s wall‑clock time.

## Timestamp Handling in Sender Reports
- Each SR includes:
- The **RTP timestamp** of the most recently generated RTP packet, reflecting the media sampling clock.
- The **full NTP timestamp**, representing the absolute wall‑clock time at which the RTP packet was generated.
- This dual‑timestamp information enables receivers to:
- Align media playback across different streams.
- Perform jitter calculations and delay compensation.

## RTCP Timestamp Synchronization

- Each RTP packet carries a **full clock timestamp** that indicates the exact time the packet was created.
- The timestamp is derived from the media samples and reflects the **real device time** at packet generation.
- By comparing the timestamps of the last packet sent with the timestamps received, the receiver can **match and link** the two streams.
- This association allows the receiver to **synchronize playback** so that audio and video are presented together at the correct moments.

> *If the synchronization fails, you may notice a “hollow” movement where the voice does not match the speaker’s lip‑movement.*

## RTCP Bandwidth Scaling

- The bandwidth allocated to RTCP control traffic is **limited** because RTCP adds overhead on top of the media stream.
- **Rule of thumb:** RTCP control traffic must not exceed **5 % of the total available bandwidth**.

### Example
| Role | Video bandwidth | 5 % of total (RTCP limit) |
|--------|----------------|---------------------------|
| Sender | 2 Mbps video | 100 kbps RTCP (5 % of 2 Mbps) |

---

## Splitting the RTCP Bandwidth

When multiple participants are present, the 5 % RTCP budget is divided as follows:
- **25 %** of the RTCP budget → **Sender**
- **75 %** of the RTCP budget → **All Receivers** (shared equally)

### Allocation per Receiver

If the total RTCP budget is 100 kbps:
| Number of Receivers | Bandwidth per Receiver |
|---------------------|------------------------|
| 10 | 7.5 kbps (75 kbps ÷ 10) |
| 5 | 15 kbps (75 kbps ÷ 5) |

The sender retains the full **25 kbps** portion.

---

## Determining the RTCP Packet Transmission Period

Each participant (sender or receiver) decides **how often** to send an RTCP packet based on:
1. **Average RTCP packet size** (bits)
2. **Allocated RTCP rate** (bits per second)

\[
\text{Transmission interval} = \frac{\text{Average packet size}}{\text{Allocated rate}}
\]

### Example Calculation

- **Average RTCP packet size:** 1 kilobit = 1 000 bits
- **Allocated RTCP bandwidth for a receiver:** 10 kilobits/s

\[
\frac{1\,000\ \text{bits}}{10\,000\ \text{bits/s}} = 0.1\ \text{s} = 100\ \text{ms}
\]

- The receiver will therefore transmit an RTCP packet **every 100 ms**.
- Sending at this rate would consume the entire allocated RTCP bandwidth; increasing the interval (e.g., to 200 ms) frees up bandwidth for other control traffic.

---

## Summary

- RTCP timestamps enable precise synchronization of audio and video streams.
- RTCP control traffic is limited to **5 %** of the total session bandwidth.
- The 5 % budget is split **25 % for the sender** and **75 % for all receivers**, with the receiver share divided equally among them.
- Participants calculate their RTCP transmission interval by dividing the **average packet size** by the **allocated RTCP rate**.
