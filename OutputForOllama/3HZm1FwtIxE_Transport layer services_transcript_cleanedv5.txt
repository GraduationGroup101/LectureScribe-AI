# Transport Layer

## Goals of the Chapter
- Multiplexing
- Flow control
- Congestion control
- Understanding UDP and TCP

## End‑to‑End Protocols
- The Transport Layer provides **logical communication** between any two devices.
- Protocols that operate in the Transport Layer are called **end‑to‑end protocols** because they exist only at the communicating hosts, not within the core network routers.
- Routers normally implement only the **Network Layer**; they do not have a Transport Layer.
- Some routers that act as gateways may include a **Transport Gateway**, but this is an exception.
- Application‑layer protocols such as **HTTP** and **SMTP** are also end‑to‑end protocols.

## Segment as the Transport‑Layer Message Unit
- The Transport Layer transmits data in units called **segments**.
- The sender takes messages from applications (e.g., Facebook, email) and **breaks them into segments**, even if the original message is large.
- Each segment will later be **encapsulated inside an IP packet** (and ultimately inside a data‑link frame).

## Sender Operations
- Application‑layer messages → **segments** (via TCP or UDP).
- Segments are prepared for transmission to the network.

## Receiver Operations (Physical Flow)
1. The segment is delivered to the **IP layer** (Network Layer).
2. The segment is **encapsulated** in an IP packet.
3. The IP packet is placed inside a **data‑link frame**.
4. The frame is transmitted as **bits and signals** over the physical medium.
5. At the destination, the reverse process occurs:
- Signals → Frame → IP packet → **decapsulation** → original **segment** (from TCP or UDP).

## Available Transport Protocols
- **TCP** (Transmission Control Protocol)
- **UDP** (User Datagram Protocol)
- Some special protocols exist for specific vendors (e.g., Apple), but they are not the focus of this material.

## Internet Case Study
- The textbook and lecture concentrate on the **Internet** as the primary example.
- The two transport protocols used on the Internet are **TCP** and **UDP**.

## Transport Layer Services
- The Transport Layer and its protocols provide **logical communication** between end hosts, supporting multiplexing, flow control, and congestion control.

## Transport Layer and Logical Communication

The transport layer provides **logical communication between processes**.
- A *process* here refers to the program and its execution context as defined by the operating system.
- Communication is therefore **program‑to‑program** (e.g., Facebook ↔ Facebook, Email ↔ Email, etc.).

In contrast, the network layer (IP) provides logical communication **between hosts** – i.e., between computer devices.

---

## Internet Transport‑Layer Protocols

The two main transport‑layer protocols are **TCP** and **UDP**.

### TCP (Transmission Control Protocol)
- **Reliability** – uses acknowledgments.
- **In‑order delivery** – employs sequence numbers to order segments.
- **Congestion control**.
- **Flow control**.
- **Connection setup** (three‑way handshake).

(Details of these mechanisms will be covered later.)

### UDP (User Datagram Protocol)
- **Unreliable** – no acknowledgments, no sequence numbers.
- **Connectionless** – provides unordered delivery.
- **No‑frills** – no extensions or add‑ons; it simply hands datagrams to IP.
- Often described as **best‑effort**, a term also used for IP service.

---

## Services Not Provided by TCP or UDP

Both TCP and UDP **do not guarantee** the following:
- **Delay guarantees** – no maximum‑delay assurance.
- **Bandwidth guarantees** – no minimum‑bandwidth assurance.

These guarantees are not part of the transport‑layer services.

---

## Application‑Layer Services for Guarantees

To obtain delay and bandwidth guarantees, newer services are defined at the **application layer**:
- **Integrated Services (IntServ)**.
- **Differentiated Services (DiffServ)**.

These mechanisms provide the missing guarantees through higher‑layer support.
